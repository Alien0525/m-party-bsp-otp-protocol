Protocol A5 (m = 4): remaining reuse cases

Snapshot of the code on 7 November 2026 (rules R1 to R19). The tracer and visualizer linked here are frozen copies of that code, so every case below reproduces exactly.

Step-by-step tracer Visualizer

Setting

Four parties share one pad of n one-time pads. P0 (moving right) and P1 (moving left) start at the two ends of the first half, [1, n/2]; P2 (right) and P3 (left) start at the two ends of the second half, [n/2+1, n]. Each message uses one pad and is broadcast to the other three. Delivery is asynchronous, but no party ever has more than d of its messages undelivered to any other party. Each party keeps a view: where it believes each other party is, and the pads it reserves for each of them (its current prediction of their next d pads) or still holds for them (earlier predictions not yet resolved). Jump spots come from a fixed BSP tree of nodes (M, M+1), visited root first.

Rules in this snapshot

Moving

  1. R1 Sending. A party uses its next pad in its direction only if that pad is not used and is not reserved or held for anyone in its view. Otherwise it jumps (R2). If there is no valid jump spot, it cannot send (see R12).
  2. R2 Jump target. The root-most BSP node (M, M+1) that is not fully occupied and passes R3. A right-mover lands on M+1, a left-mover on M, and the direction stays the same.
  3. R3 Jump check. A node is valid only if there are more than 2d free pads on both sides of it, counted outside the node. Pads that are used, believed positions, and pads reserved or held for others all count as taken, so the check runs from the furthest reserved pad of each party, not from its believed position. On the lander's back side, pads reserved only for parties moving away from the node do not count.

Predicting the others

  1. R4 Recompute. Predictions are recomputed whenever anything in the view changes.
  2. R5 Disjoint. A pad reserved for one party is not reserved for another; when predicting a party, the pads reserved for the others count as obstacles.
  3. R6 Only valid jump spots. A jump spot is predicted only while it passes R3. Once it stops passing, it is no longer predicted (a pad already reserved stays held under R9).
  4. R7 Refresh everyone. On every received message, the predictions for all parties are refreshed, not only for the sender.
  5. R8 Message order. Messages carry a per-sender number. (a) An older message only marks its pad used and never moves the sender back. (b) A newer message that skips k numbers means the sender used k pads on the way, and those pads are marked as used: the reserved ones if it provably followed them, the straight-line ones if it provably walked, and every pad it could have used if it jumped unexpectedly.
  6. R9 Held pads. A predicted pad stays held until all of that party's messages up to the one it was predicted for have arrived (then it is known used or known unused). Held pads are never used (R1).
  7. R13 Legal landings only. A party never predicts another party to land on one of its own next d pads, and all predictions are recomputed after its own move.
  8. R14 Two timelines. When two parties approach head-on and their predicted pads overlap, the order in which they meet is unknown, so an observer reserves both futures for each of them: walking on, and jumping (with the jump spot reserved).
  9. R15 Own reservation. Each party also reserves its own next d pads (walking, or its jump spot and the pad after it), in both timelines, in the fixed priority order P0, P1, P2, P3. Lower-priority parties are never predicted to land on them.

Held jump pads

  1. R16 Free when the jump is known. Pads held for X from earlier jump predictions are freed once the observer knows for sure where X jumped: it has X's messages up to and including the jump, with none missing.
  2. R17 Hold the pad after a jump spot. When an observer holds a predicted landing pad for X, it also holds the d − 1 pads after it in X's direction, where X walks right after landing.
  3. R18 Keep until the jump is known. Held jump pads are not released just because X's next message turned out to be a walk, since X may still make that jump one message later. They are released only by R16, or when the pad is used.
  4. R19 Shared pad. If a pad an observer predicts for X is also reserved or held for another party, X may have to step around it, so the observer also reserves that way around (usually a jump).

Stopping

  1. R12 Halt-all. If any party cannot send, the whole protocol halts and all remaining pads count as wasted.

R10 (shared slots by priority) and R11 (keep your own next pad) were tried earlier and removed, because they caused reuses.

What the 25 cases have in common

In every case the two parties move in the same direction, and the pad that gets reused is the landing pad of the first party's jump. The second party had the wrong idea about that jump: whether it would happen, or where it would land.

They disagree because each party's view of the others is a little out of date (up to d messages), and reservations made earlier stay held. All 25 happen late in the run, when free space is broken into small pieces and many parties need to jump. They split into three groups:

GroupRuns
1. Both jump to the same spot, each leaving the best spot to the other12
2. A party lands where the other is heading, because the other's picture of it is out of date8
3. A jump the other party did not expect at all4
Other1

1. Each leaves the best spot to the other

Example
n = 256, d = 2, seed 39, delivery adversarial, sender order arena-skew. P2 reuses pad 169 at step 211.
Open in the tracer

P0 and P2 both move right, and both are about to jump.

  1. In P0's view, P2 will jump to 177, so P0 reserves 177 for P2.
  2. In P2's view, P0 will jump to 177, so P2 reserves 177 for P0.
  3. At step 210 P0 jumps. 177 is reserved for P2, so it takes the next spot, 169.
  4. At step 211 P2 jumps. 177 is reserved for P0, so it also takes 169.

Each party keeps the best spot free for the other, and both land on the second choice. Seed 26 (adversarial, silent1) is the same pattern, where one side keeps the spot free because of priority and the other because of an earlier held reservation.

2. Out-of-date picture of the lander

Example
n = 256, d = 2, seed 63, delivery worst, sender order greedy-gap. P0 reuses pad 169 at step 218.
Open in the tracer
  1. P0 has already jumped from 234 to 161, but that message has not reached P2. P2 still believes P0 is at 234, about to jump to 177, and keeps 177 free for it.
  2. P2 needs to jump, so at step 210 it takes the next spot, 169.
  3. P0 knows it is at 161 and not going to 177, so it predicts P2 will jump to 177 and reserves 177 for it (R19 makes sure that jump is reserved at all).
  4. P0 walks from 161 up to 168, and at step 218 steps onto 169.

The extra reservation is placed on the spot P0 thinks P2 will choose, but P2 chooses from an out-of-date picture of P0.

3. A jump the other did not expect

Example
n = 256, d = 2, seed 28, delivery randcap, sender order arena-skew. P1 reuses pad 200 at step 221.
Open in the tracer
  1. P3 moves left and is at 238. In P3's view, its next pad 237 is held for P0 (an earlier prediction of P0's jump). It cannot step on it, so at step 217 it jumps to 200.
  2. P1 never made that prediction for P0, so it does not hold 237 and expects P3 simply to walk to 237 and 236. It reserves nothing at 200.
  3. When P1 itself has to jump, 200 looks free, and at step 221 it lands there.

The two parties remember different old predictions about a third party, so they disagree about whether P3 can walk on.

What we tried that did not help

  • Reserving every party's jump spot in advance, even when it is not about to jump: fixed some cases but gave more reuses overall (23 in the 2,000-run sample) and lower efficiency.
  • Strict priority for jump spots (a higher-priority party ignores the jump pads it holds for lower-priority parties when choosing its own): fixed seed 26 and others in group 1, but overall 33 of 219 still reuse and 5 in the sample.
  • Assuming others still see me at my old position (aimed at group 2): together with strict priority, 39 of 219 and 10 in the sample. The extra reservations block the party's own walk, so it jumps when nobody expects it.

A pattern across these attempts: any reservation that one party makes and the others do not make the same way can block that party's own walk and produce a jump nobody predicted, which is group 3 again.
