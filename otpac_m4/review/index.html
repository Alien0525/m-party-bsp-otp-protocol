Protocol A5 (m = 4): remaining reuse cases

Snapshot of the code on 6 November 2026. The tracer and visualizer linked here are frozen copies of that code, so every case below reproduces exactly.

Step-by-step tracer Visualizer

Rules in this snapshot

  • R1 to R9 as in the paper, with the jump check (R3) on both sides of the node: a landing needs more than 2d free pads on each side. Pads another party has reserved or held count as taken, so the check runs from that party's furthest reserved pad, not from its believed position.
  • R12 (halt-all): if any party cannot send, the protocol halts and all remaining pads count as wasted.
  • R13 (legal landings only): a party never predicts another party to land on one of its own next d pads. Every prediction is recomputed after each move. Earlier predictions stay in the held list and are never used (R1).
  • R14 (two timelines): when two parties approach head-on, each observer reserves both possible futures for each of them, walking on or jumping.
  • R15 (own reservation): each party also reserves its own next d pads, in both timelines, in the fixed priority order P0, P1, P2, P3. Lower-priority parties are never predicted to land on them.

That is 31 distinct runs with a reuse (one run is in both sets). In all of them, the delivery bound held throughout: no party ever had more than d undelivered messages. No party ever wrote a pad that was reserved in its own view.

Efficiency is lower than under the old rules mainly because of R12: at small n, one halt wastes a large share of the pad.

What the 31 cases have in common

In every case, two parties disagree about whether and where one of them jumps. While the jump message is still in flight (it is one of the d messages allowed to be undelivered), the other party moves onto the same pad.

They disagree because each party's view of the others is a little out of date, and reservations made earlier stay held. So two views can block different pads, and two parties can compute different jump spots for the same party, or one expects a walk where the other jumps.

All 31 happen late in the run (around step 180 or later for n = 256), when free space is broken into small pieces and many parties need to jump. They split into three patterns:

PatternRuns
A. Two parties moving the same direction jump to the same spot16
B. A party walks into the spot where a same-direction party just landed13
C. Two parties moving toward each other, one of them just after a jump2

A. Both jump to the same spot

Example
n = 256, d = 2, seed 26, delivery adversarial, sender order silent1. P1 reuses pad 16 at step 189.
Open in the tracer

P1 and P3 both move left. P0 never sends and stays at pad 1.

  1. Around step 182, P1 is still walking (at 73). It predicts that P3 will soon have to jump, to 32, and reserves 32 for P3. That pad then stays in P1's held list.
  2. At step 187 P1 is at 72. P2 is walking toward it, so P1 must jump. In its own view, 32 is still held for P3, so P1 picks the next spot, 16.
  3. P3 has learned that P1 must jump. P1 has priority, so P3 gives 32 to P1 and takes 16 itself. At step 188 P3 jumps to 16.
  4. At step 189 P1 jumps to 16 as well.

Each party gives 32 to the other: P3 because of priority, P1 because of the earlier reservation it keeps. Both land on the second choice.

B. Walking into a fresh landing

Example
n = 256, d = 2, seed 47, delivery worst, sender order greedy-gap. P1 reuses pad 40 at step 228.
Open in the tracer
  1. P3 walks left down to 51. In P3's view, pads 49 and 50 are held for P0 (an earlier prediction of P0's jump). So P3 cannot walk on, and at step 220 it jumps to 40.
  2. P1 is at 48, behind that spot and also moving left. P1 does not hold 49 and 50 for P0, so it expects P3 simply to walk on to 51 and 50.
  3. When P3 jumped, the node passed the both-sides check: P1's believed position and reservations were more than 2d away.
  4. P3 then does not send again, and its last two messages (51 and the jump to 40) do not reach P1, which the bound allows. P1 keeps walking, from 48 down to 41, still believing P3 is at 52. At step 228 it steps onto 40.

The 2d clearance protects the landing at the moment of the jump. It does not stop a walker that does not know about the jump from walking up to it later, if the lander stays silent.

C. Head-on, right after a jump

Example
n = 256, d = 2, seed 98, delivery adversarial, sender order skew. P1 reuses pad 162 at step 180.
Open in the tracer
  1. P1 moves left through the area around 160 to 170. While P0 is at 16, P1 predicts P0 will walk to 17 and then jump to 161, so it reserves 161 for P0.
  2. At step 172 P0 jumps from 17 to 161. In P0's view, P1 is still far enough away for the node to pass the check.
  3. That message has not reached P1. At step 173 P1 is at 168, close enough that node (160,161) no longer passes the check in its view, so it now predicts P0 will jump to 225. Pad 161 stays held for P0, but pad 162, where P0 walks right after landing on 161, was never reserved.
  4. At step 178 P0 walks to 162. Neither of its last two messages has reached P1, which still believes P0 is at 17. At step 180 P1 walks from 163 onto 162.

Open question

In all three patterns, a held reservation covers the jump spot one party predicted earlier, but not the spot the jumper actually picks from its own view. Two directions we have looked at:

  • Make every party compute the same jump spot (for example, strict priority applied to held reservations too). This would fix pattern A, but it means sometimes freeing a held reservation.
  • Have every party reserve every party's jump spot in advance, even when it is not about to jump. We tested this: it fixed some cases but caused more reuses overall (23 in the 2,000-run sample) and lower efficiency, so it is not in this snapshot.
